How far back can we go — and why Standedge?

Research reviewed 4 November 2026. Free sources only; no registration or payment.

The short answer

The earliest Bentley currently identified in the Saddleworth parish material is Mathew, father of An, baptised 1 May 1613. This is evidence of a family already there, not of an arrival in 1613. It is not yet a proven ancestor-to-descendant chain from that man to Roy.

The underlying baptism citations are in the earlier reconstruction and the Saddleworth research. Dates of baptism are not dates of birth. A father active in 1613 was necessarily born earlier, but that does not justify inventing a birth year or parents.

Why the records run out

Mike Buckley's article explains that the earliest surviving local register book begins in 1632. Earlier entries were recovered from annual returns sent to the diocese. Lost sheets, gaps and discoloured writing limit the surviving evidence. A 1612 return, formerly misfiled as Whitworth, was later identified. Source: SHS Bulletin 42(4), Winter 2012, pp. 117–118, article and transcription, citing Lancashire Archives DRM 2/297.

I checked that published 1612 transcription: it contains no Bentley entry. It lists events, not every resident. A family without a baptism, marriage or burial that year would be absent. This does not show that Mathew arrived in 1613.

Likewise, the earlier note's proposed migration window of 1379–1613 is too confident. Absence from a tax list cannot establish that a family was absent from the district. Nor does a sparse online baptism index prove that the original pages are missing: we must distinguish a gap in surviving records from a gap in transcription coverage.

Was he running from something?

There is no evidence establishing a flight into Saddleworth, an original home, or even which generation moved there. “The first Bentley we can find” and “the first Bentley to settle there” are different questions.

The first is in J. P. Earwaker, editor, The Constables' Accounts of the Manor of Manchester, vol. I (1891), p. 69, scan, PDF p. 95. The 1620 dating follows the account-year heading; the entry itself says 22 September. Consulted through the printed transcription/OCR, not the manuscript.

The second is reproduced by Howard C. Lambert, “Saddleworth records from the seventeenth century”, SHS Bulletin 21(1), p. 12, scan, PDF p. 15. The printed page was visually inspected. It cites West Riding Session Records, Yorkshire Archaeological Society, volume 54. The original sessions record has not been inspected.

These men are unlinked research candidates, not added ancestors. The earliest parish evidence also predates the 1638/9 order by 25 years. That later incident cannot, by itself, explain the family's first recorded presence.

Why live in that landscape?

The hills can look isolated today, but dispersed farms were part of a working economy. An Oldham Council historical account describes families combining farming with domestic woollen manufacture and carrying cloth to Huddersfield by packhorse. The Royal Geographical Society's Saddleworth walk explains the established route network and the useful position of hillside settlements between wet valley bottoms and steeper ground.

Our eighteenth-century records repeatedly describe the Bentleys as clothiers. Work, land, tenancy and family connections therefore offer plausible explanations for living there. This is contextual inference, not a discovered motive for the earliest Mathew. We cannot project the later clothier occupation onto a seventeenth-century namesake without evidence. Canals and railways also came too late to explain the 1613 family.

The 1791 “Bentleys” deeds show a house, land and financial transactions. They are useful evidence of a settled local economy, but their John and James have not been securely placed in this pedigree. See the deed references.

The best ways further back

The SHS archive catalogue, H/KID, Leonard Kidd Collection, printed p. 248, lists copies of Jonas Bentley of Standedge's 1686 administration and inventory dated 18 August, and Matthew of Standedge's will dated 18 November 1751, proved 3 March 1753. These are specific surviving leads; the catalogue does not reveal their family relationships. It also distinguishes John of Diggle's administration, 18 February 1713/14, from John of Boarshurst's later will.

Priorities are to locate freely accessible transcriptions of these documents; compare property descriptions and witnesses; check manorial rentals, court rolls and earlier tax returns; and test the legal-record Mathews against the parish households. Missing links must be resolved before attaching earlier names. We have reached an evidence boundary, not demonstrated that earlier generations can never be found.
