Mathewe Bentley · Saddleworth, Manchester and York, 1619–58

The road to York Castle

Two legal records from the seventeenth century carry the name Mathew Bentley far beyond the churchyard at St Chad’s. The first sits in the Manchester constables’ accounts for 1619–20. On 22 September, the constables paid twopence to send on a hue and cry for Henry Platte, who had wounded Mathew Bentley of Saddleworth so badly that he was “in great danger of death”. Word of a fight in the Pennine hills had ridden down into Lancashire with a description of the suspect’s clothes.

Nineteen winters later, in January 1638/9, the West Riding Quarter Sessions recorded a very different order. A Mathewe Bentley, described as late of Saddleworth, labourer, had refused to become bound with sureties to appear at the next sessions and keep the peace in the meantime. The justices ordered him committed to York Castle. Whether he was the father who had baptised An in 1613 and young Mathew in 1614, or that son now grown to twenty-four, the court book does not say.

What comes next makes the timing striking. In February 1641/2, every adult man in Saddleworth was called upon to sign the Protestation return, and not a single Bentley appears on the list. Only in the 1650s does the surname return to the St Chad’s register—when Jonas Bentley baptised his son John in August 1654, and when an old “Mathey” Bentley was finally buried on 8 February 1657/8. The court roll cannot prove that the prisoner at York Castle was our ancestor, but it explains how a Saddleworth labourer could vanish from the parish rolls and leave a silence behind him.
