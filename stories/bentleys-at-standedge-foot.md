# The house called Bentleys, and the cousin at Woodmans

John, James and Matthew Bentley · Standedge Foot, Bleak Hey Nook and Woodmans, 1779–1822

For generations the parish clerks of Saddleworth wrote *Stone-edge* or *Standedge* beside the Bentleys’ names as if the whole wind-swept ridge were a single doorway. Only when we lay the deeds, newspaper notices and manuscript maps of 1791 to 1822 side by side do the actual roofs and stone walls come into focus.

High up the turnpike road toward the Standedge cutting, where Burk Clough drops down from Millstone Edge near Thieves Bridge, stood an old wayside building called **Standedge Foot**. Built in 1680, it had once been an inn where coach drivers watered their horses before tackling the long pull up Standedge Hill. Immediately beside it at Rough stood a holding of two cottages and eight acres of pasture that the whole district knew simply by the family’s name: **Bentleys**. Lower down the hillside, along Whimberry Lee Lane, stood **Bleak Hey Nook**, where the mid-eighteenth-century stone weavers’ house still called Bentley Farmhouse stands today.

Friday, 26 November 1791, was a remarkable day in the family’s history. On that day, William Farrer, lord of the manor of Saddleworth, sealed the release conveying the freehold of **“Bentleys”** and its eight acres to two Bentley clothiers: **John Bentley of Bentleys** and **James Bentley of Blakehey Nook**. Fifteen years later, in May 1806, John and James sold the estate to their neighbour Joseph Scholfield of Blakehey Nook, a drysalter, yet the old name stuck so fast that the **1822 Map of Saddleworth** still lettered **BENTLEYS** right beside **STANEDGE FOOT**.

On that very same Friday—26 November 1791—an auctioneer’s hammer fell on another Saddleworth farm called **Woodmans**, down near Delph. The sale notice in the *Manchester Mercury* describes Woodmans as a newly erected dwelling house lately converted into **two dwellings**, with an ancient cottage, a shippon called the Park-Coat, and nearly twenty acres of meadow and clough. The two men sharing those two dwellings at Woodmans as under-tenants were **Matthew Bentley** and **Thomas Rhodes**.

Why were Matthew Bentley and Thomas Rhodes living under the same divided roof at Woodmans? The parish register holds the human key. Twelve years earlier, on 4 April 1779, **Esther Bentley, spinster of Standedge**—daughter of James and Mary Bentley—had brought a baby boy named William to be christened at St Chad’s, and the clerk had written down the father’s name as **Thomas Rhodes**. Matthew Bentley, baptised in January 1738/9, was Esther’s uncle. Finding Uncle Matthew and Thomas Rhodes sharing the two-dwelling farmhouse at Woodmans in 1791 turns a dry real-estate advertisement into a glimpse of family solidarity: twelve years after an unmarried Standedge daughter bore Thomas Rhodes’s child, her uncle and that child’s father were farming the same twenty acres side by side.
