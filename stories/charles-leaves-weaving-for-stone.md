# When Charles put down the wool

Charles Bentley, Sarah Dyson and their children · Marsden, 1851–97

At ten years old, John Bentley already had a trade to give the census taker. He was a woollen piecer at Binn, joining broken threads. His sister Elizabeth, fifteen, wound bobbins. Their father Charles, who knew a weaver’s work as well as anyone in that house, was by then earning his living from stone.

Ten years earlier Charles and his wife Sarah Dyson had been at Carr Clough with Elizabeth, Rebecca and baby John. Charles was weaving woollens, and so was his sister Ann, who lived with them. For little John, the trade he would enter was there in the family from the time he was three months old.

Binn itself was changing. In November 1870 Huddersfield’s town clerk gave notice of plans to widen and divert the road from Wessenden reservoir to Binn Lane. The next spring, when Charles gave his occupation as stone delver, that proposal was barely five months old. The place where he worked the stone is still unknown.

Between those two visits by the census taker, Charles had moved house and changed trades. By 1871 his work at Binn was digging or quarrying stone. Textile work went on in the same household, now in the hands of his children. Sarah died in 1874, when the youngest boy, Charles E., was still small. Charles lived until 1897. By then the ten-year-old piecer had grown up, married and become a woollen weaver with children of his own.
